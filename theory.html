Quantitative Frameworks

Theory & Mathematical Models

Formal mathematical and computational models that describe how individuals value delayed rewards and make intertemporal decisions.

Intertemporal Choice

Delay Discounting Models

Mathematical models describe how the subjective value of a reward decreases as a function of delay. Different formulations capture distinct patterns of human discounting behavior.

Exponential Discounting

The simplest model assumes constant discounting rate—rewards lose value at a fixed percentage per unit time. Despite its theoretical elegance, it poorly captures human behavior.

V = A × e-kD
Standard Form (Continuous Time)
Where: V = present value of delayed reward | A = reward amount | k = discounting rate parameter | D = delay in time units | e = Euler's number (≈2.718)

Limitation

Exponential models predict that time preference is constant over time (time consistency). However, humans exhibit preference reversals, preferring to wait 10 vs. 11 weeks more readily than choosing between immediate vs. 1 week delayed rewards.

Hyperbolic Discounting (Mazur)

Observed discounting is better described by a hyperbolic function, which predicts steeper discounting at shorter delays and shallower discounting at longer delays. This captures the "present bias" phenomenon.

V = A / (1 + kD)
Mazur's Hyperbolic Model
Where: V = present value | A = reward magnitude | k = discounting rate parameter | D = delay

Mathematical Properties

  • Near delay: Value drops steeply with small increases in delay
  • Far delay: Additional delays have minimal impact on value
  • Time-inconsistency: Explains preference reversals and self-control problems
  • Better empirical fit: Consistently outperforms exponential models

Quasi-Hyperbolic Discounting (Beta-Delta)

Also called the Laibson model, this combines exponential and hyperbolic features. It includes an immediate discount factor (β) and exponential time-discounting (δ), capturing both present bias and constant long-term patience.

V = A × (β × δD)    [for D > 0]
Quasi-Hyperbolic Model
Where: β = immediate discount factor (0 ≤ β ≤ 1, typically < 1 indicating present bias) | δ = exponential discount factor per unit time | D = delay

Hyperboloid Model (Loewenstein & Prelec)

This generalized function includes a shape parameter (s) that allows flexible curvature. When s = 1, it reduces to the Mazur hyperbolic model; when s → 0, it approaches exponential discounting.

V = A / (1 + kD)s
Generalized Hyperboloid Model
Where: s = shape parameter | When s > 1, the function has an even steeper initial drop; when 0 < s < 1, the drop is shallower. The exponent s captures individual differences in discounting curvature.

Key Finding

The choice of discount model affects parameter interpretation and clinical implications. Hyperbolic models better predict real-world behavior and explain why people struggle with long-term commitments despite valuing future outcomes.

Economic Behavior

Behavioral Economic Framework

Demand curve analysis quantifies how much of a reinforcer (drug, food, money) is consumed as its "price" or cost increases.

Essential Demand Parameters

Behavioral economic demand curves characterize consumption across price levels using three key parameters:

  • Amplitude (Q0): Consumption at zero price (y-intercept); reflects initial motivation or desire for the commodity
  • Persistence (α): How steeply consumption decreases with price increases; captures essential value and difficulty substituting the commodity
  • Essential Value (Pmax): The theoretical price at which demand reaches zero; indicates maximum willingness to pay or effort expended

Exponential Demand Equation

log Q = log Q₀ + k(e-α·P - 1)
Exponential Demand Model
Where: Q = quantity consumed at price P | Q₀ = consumption at zero price | α = elasticity parameter | k = scaling constant | e = Euler's number

This equation characterizes "normal" commodity demand. Steeper slopes indicate more essential goods (addictive substances show steep, inelastic demand).

Clinical Application

Demand curve analysis predicts treatment response in addiction. Individuals with high essential value (steep demand curves) are at greater relapse risk. Price increases (e.g., taxation) effectively reduce consumption for less essential commodities but may have limited impact on highly essential drugs.

Multidimensional Trait Model

UPPS-P Model of Impulsivity

Rather than treating impulsivity as a unitary construct, the UPPS-P model identifies five distinct dimensions that predict different outcomes and have different neural and genetic substrates.

Negative Urgency

The tendency to act rashly in response to negative emotions. Associated with:

  • Neural: Amygdala reactivity, prefrontal-limbic dysfunction
  • Genetic: COMT, serotonergic variants
  • Outcomes: Emotion-driven substance use, self-harm

Positive Urgency

Rash action in response to positive emotions or excitement. Associated with:

  • Neural: Reward system hyperactivity, ventral striatum
  • Genetic: Dopaminergic variants (DRD2, DAT1)
  • Outcomes: Reward-driven risk-taking, binge consumption

Sensation Seeking

Preference for exciting, novel, and intense experiences. Associated with:

  • Neural: Low baseline dopamine, reward-seeking activation
  • Genetic: Low COMT activity, low dopamine in baseline state
  • Outcomes: Drug experimentation, novelty-seeking behaviors

Lack of Premeditation

Failure to consider consequences before acting. Associated with:

  • Neural: Reduced prefrontal-striatal connectivity, weak foresight
  • Genetic: Variants affecting executive function networks
  • Outcomes: Careless errors, planning failures, accidents

Lack of Perseverance

Difficulty maintaining focus and effort toward goals. Associated with:

  • Neural: Anterior cingulate dysfunction, salience network
  • Genetic: Variants affecting sustained attention and effort
  • Outcomes: Task switching, goal abandonment, ADHD symptoms

Delay Discounting (Implicit)

The UPPS-P focuses on affective impulsivity; delay discounting represents a separate "cold" cognitive dimension that sometimes correlates with these factors.

  • Neural: Ventromedial prefrontal cortex, valuation systems
  • Genetic: Partly distinct from UPPS-P dimensions
  • Outcomes: Temporal preference, intertemporal choice

Clinical Relevance

Different impulsivity dimensions predict distinct clinical outcomes. Negative urgency predicts externalizing disorders and substance use; sensation seeking predicts early drug experimentation; lack of perseverance predicts ADHD. Interventions should target the specific impulsivity dimension driving problematic behavior.

Developmental Theory

Life Course Perspective

Impulsivity and self-regulation show systematic developmental changes. Understanding these trajectories reveals critical periods and mechanisms of change across the lifespan.

Adolescence (10-25 years)

Characterized by heightened impulsivity, reward sensitivity, and risk-taking. Prefrontal cortex development lags limbic system development, creating a "developmental mismatch."

  • Peak impulsivity: mid-to-late adolescence
  • Sensation seeking increases
  • Substance use vulnerability is highest
  • Gradual prefrontal maturation begins

Early Adulthood (25-40 years)

Continued myelination and pruning of prefrontal systems. Impulsivity generally decreases. Social/economic roles increase self-regulation demands.

  • Prefrontal maturation largely complete
  • Impulsivity stabilizes at adult levels
  • Career and family milestones increase consistency
  • Genetic effects more pronounced

Later Adulthood (40+ years)

Gradual cognitive decline, but emotional regulation often improves. Impulsivity trajectories become more variable due to health and social factors.

  • Executive functions decline
  • Emotional regulation paradoxically improves
  • Health conditions affect impulsivity
  • Individual differences increase

Longitudinal Research: Large Cohort Studies

ABCD Study (Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development)

  • N ~10,000 youth ages 9-10
  • Follows brain development from childhood through early adulthood
  • Directly measures delay discounting and behavioral impulsivity
  • Tracks substance use initiation and outcomes
  • Reveals protective factors in brain development

ALSPAC (Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children)

  • N ~15,000 participants born in 1991-1992
  • Longest-running UK birth cohort study
  • Comprehensive behavioral and genetic assessment
  • Tracks impulsivity changes from childhood through adulthood
  • Models gene-environment interactions

Key Finding: Age-Specific Genetic Architecture

Genetic influences on impulsivity vary across development. SNPs predicting high impulsivity in adolescence may have attenuated effects in adulthood. This suggests that genes act through developmental mechanisms (e.g., prefrontal maturation timing) rather than static traits.

Dual-Process Theory

Competing Neurobehavioral Decision Systems (CNDS)

The CNDS framework proposes that impulsive and controlled decision-making arise from two neurobiologically distinct systems that compete for behavioral control.

System 1: Approach System

Automatic, rapid, emotion-driven decision-making

Characteristics

  • Ventral striatum, ventromedial prefrontal cortex
  • Rapid, parallel processing
  • Driven by immediate reward/threat
  • Emotionally valenced decisions
  • Minimal cognitive effort required

Behavioral Signature

  • High temporal discounting
  • Risk-seeking behavior
  • Preference for immediate gratification
  • Strong response to incentives

System 2: Control System

Deliberate, effortful, goal-oriented decision-making

Characteristics

  • Lateral prefrontal cortex, parietal regions
  • Serial, sequential processing
  • Driven by long-term goals/values
  • Rational, logical decisions
  • Requires sustained attention/effort

Behavioral Signature

  • Low temporal discounting
  • Risk-averse behavior
  • Preference for delayed gratification
  • Consistency with stated values

System Competition & Behavioral Outcomes

System 1 Dominates

Approach system overpowers control system. High motivation, low inhibition.

  • Substance use
  • Risky sexual behavior
  • Aggressive outbursts
  • Pathological gambling

Balanced Competition

Systems engage in dynamic competition. Outcome depends on context and effort.

  • Healthy decision-making
  • Contextual impulse control
  • Flexible self-regulation
  • Adaptive risk-taking

System 2 Dominates

Control system overpowers approach system. Rigid self-control, excessive inhibition.

  • Overcontrol
  • Anhedonia
  • Obsessive tendencies
  • Anxiety disorders

Intervention Implications

The CNDS framework suggests interventions should work on both systems: strengthen system 2 (cognitive training, goal clarification) and reduce system 1 activation (cue exposure, stress management, reducing incentive salience). Effective self-regulation requires both enhanced control capacity and reduced approach motivation.

Mathematical Models Guide Scientific Understanding

By formalizing theoretical concepts into mathematical equations, we can make precise predictions about behavior, identify key parameters that predict individual differences, and test competing theories. These models bridge the gap between brain biology and observable behavior, enabling translational research that improves interventions for impulse control disorders.
